# Reviewer Comments

## Reviewer 1

**Recommendation:** Publish as is; no revisions needed.

### Summary

The reviewer finds that the manuscript is well written and settles long-standing controversies in the field. They recommend publication as is, with two minor comments.

### Comments

Using a frequency-modulation atomic-force-microscopy setup, the authors simultaneously measure slip length and surface topography with nanoscale lateral resolution on different substrate types. The extracted slip lengths on flat substrates are found to be almost zero, in agreement with scaling analysis combined with molecular dynamics simulation predictions. Large apparent slip lengths are explained in terms of surface bubbles. The paper is well written and settles some long-standing controversies in the field.

1. The prefactor `C` of Eq. 2 has units of length and is given by `C = 0.41 nm`.

2. The scaling law in Eq. 2 was derived for non-polar surfaces that interact with water only via dispersion or Lennard-Jones interactions. In that model, hydrophilic surfaces were created by large dispersion attraction. In the recent publication "Subnanometer Interfacial Hydrodynamics: Spatially Resolved Viscosity and Surface Friction", Carlson et al., *Nano Lett.* 2025, 25, 15605-15612, MD simulations were used to model polar surfaces where small contact angles are created by polar surface groups, which is more realistic. For those systems, the friction coefficient is better represented by an exponential fit rather than a power law. This does not make a difference for the non-polar surfaces that are the authors' main focus, but it could be relevant for future studies.

## Reviewer 2

**Recommendation:** Publish after major revisions noted.

### Summary

The reviewer finds the article interesting and potentially suitable for Nano Letters, but states that several critical points require careful consideration and revision before publication.

### General Comments

This is an interesting article dealing with experimental measurements of water slip length on various surfaces, concluding that water need not slip on hydrophobic surfaces. The measurements use frequency-modulation atomic force microscopy (AFM), with claims of a 159-fold improvement in measurement sensitivity. The authors report a large slip length of 43.2 nm on graphite in deionized water. The reviewer notes that the manuscript may be of interest to a broad cross-section of researchers in nanoscience and nanotechnology.

### Required Revisions

1. **Title accuracy**

   The current title is not wholly accurate. The word "Does" should be replaced with "Need". The suggested title is:

   > "Water Need Not Slip on Hydrophobic Surfaces: Insights from Slip Length Mapping"

   The reviewer argues that because water does slip on graphite, the authors cannot generally state that water does not slip on hydrophobic surfaces. The claim should instead be that water need not slip on hydrophobic surfaces.

2. **Comparison with Secchi et al.**

   The authors need to contextualize their results relative to Secchi et al.:

   https://www.nature.com/articles/nature19315

   Secchi et al. reported massive radius-dependent water slippage in carbon nanotubes (CNTs), with slip lengths on the order of hundreds of nanometers. The authors should explain why massive slippage occurs in CNTs but not on the surfaces examined in this manuscript.

3. **Derivation of Eqs. 3 and 4**

   The derivation of Eqs. 3 and 4 must be provided. The symbol `hdot` must also be explained in the main text, including how it is measured experimentally.

4. **Functional form of `f*` in Eq. 5**

   The authors state that the slip length `bs` is obtained by fitting Eq. 5 to a damping-coefficient-tip-sample distance curve with `bs` as the fitting parameter. However, the functional form of `f*` in Eq. 5 is not provided. This omission must be clarified, and the form of `f*` must be derived and included in the paper.

5. **Goodness of fit and error bars in Figure 2a**

   For the fit in Figure 2a, provide a goodness-of-fit metric, such as:

   - `R^2`
   - Mean absolute error
   - Another appropriate metric

   The authors must also explain how the error bars on reported slip lengths were obtained. Specifically, clarify whether they are confidence intervals from the fit, standard deviations, standard errors, or obtained from repeated measurements.

6. **Fit plot for tip slip-length calibration**

   The authors state:

   > "We calibrated the slip length of the probe tip by measuring a symmetric system (`bt = bs`) using an atmospheric-plasma-treated diamond-like carbon (DLC) substrate, yielding a slip length of `bt = bs = 0.0 +/- 1.2 nm`."

   The reviewer asks the authors to show the fit plot for this case, similar to Figure 2a.

7. **Fit plot for nanobubble slip length**

   The authors should show the plots used to extract the slip length of 345.3 nm above the nanobubble, as mentioned near the end of page 8.

8. **Negative slip lengths**

   Explain why negative slip lengths are obtained on the flat silica and FOPA regions. The reviewer asks what the physical meaning of a negative slip length is and whether the lowest possible slip length should be 0.

9. **Teflon vs HOPG slip lengths**

   Explain why the water slip length is 0.8 nm on Teflon but 43.2 nm on HOPG, despite Teflon being expected to have a higher water contact angle than HOPG.

10. **Evidence for nanoscopic flatness**

    Figure 4 presents a systematic comparison of slip lengths on nanoscopically flat substrates. The authors must prove that these surfaces are nanoscopically flat, using evidence such as AFM measurements.

11. **Units and comparison table for Eq. 2**

    Specify the units of `b` in Eq. 2. Also provide a table comparing:

    - Calculated `b` from Eq. 2.
    - Measured `b` from experiments.

    This table should include all surfaces examined in the work.

12. **Claim about maximum contact angle on smooth flat surfaces**

    The authors need to justify the claim that a water contact angle of 120 degrees is the near-maximum achievable on a topographically smooth flat surface. The reviewer notes that Ref. 21 states:

    > "This value is considered to be the lowest surface free energy of any solid, based on the hexagonal closed alignment of -CF3 groups on the surface."

    Therefore, for surfaces with groups other than `CF3`, the contact angle could be higher. The reviewer also notes that the presence of `CF3` groups would make the surface topographically rough compared with graphite.

13. **Timing of contact-angle measurements**

    The manuscript states:

    > "The contact angles of deionized-water droplets, measured separately on the hydrophilic and hydrophobic surfaces, were 28 degrees and 105 degrees, respectively."

    The authors should explain how quickly these measurements were performed, because exposure to the atmosphere can change the contact angle, particularly for hydrophilic surfaces.

14. **Consensus on hydrophilic vs hydrophobic slip lengths**

    The authors need to substantiate the claim:

    > "Compared with hydrophilic surfaces (i.e., contact angles < 90 degree), there is no clear consensus on slip lengths for hydrophobic surfaces (i.e., contact angles > 90 degree)."

    They should also state what consensus exists in the field for hydrophilic surfaces and provide suitable references.

15. **Hydrophilicity of pristine graphite**

    The reviewer notes that pristine graphite has been shown to be hydrophilic in the last decade:

    https://pubs.acs.org/doi/full/10.1021/acs.accounts.6b00447

    The authors should explain the relatively large water slip length observed on HOPG. The reviewer asks whether it is due to hydrophobic molecules adsorbed on the surface, or due to nanobubbles, as speculated elsewhere in the paper.
